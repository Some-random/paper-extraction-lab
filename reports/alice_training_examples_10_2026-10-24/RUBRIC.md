# Selection and assessment rubric for the ten examples

The collection retains its original five selection criteria: a supported answer, fair input, a reusable biological lesson, clear reasoning, and variety. The revisions make those criteria more explicit in response to student feedback. Reference answers illustrate defensible reasoning; they are not the only acceptable wording or judgment.

## Original criteria and feedback

- **Supported answer:** Distinguish supplied observations, established background knowledge, and inference. A source-backed observation does not establish every proposed mechanism. Accept compatible explanations when the evidence does not identify a unique cause.
- **Fair input:** Supply the evidence needed for the stated task. Remove unused sequences and incidental numbers. Replace fixed choices that reward cautious wording or restrict a broader research decision with justified open responses.
- **Reusable biological lesson:** Require a consequential distinction: transferring evidence across conditions, interpreting a measurement, weighing objectives, or separating a sequence hypothesis from demonstrated function. A numerical read-off alone is insufficient for the main lesson.
- **Clear reasoning:** Reward the decisive evidence and its implication, with a specific limitation. Explanation length, generic caveats, and exhaustive lists do not earn credit by themselves.
- **Variety:** Preserve both foundational sequence interpretation and research judgment. Questions 4–8 are foundational exercises; they are not presented as advanced reasoning. Question 1 assesses comparative transfer rather than merely arithmetic.

## What each item assesses

| Item | Purpose | Evidence needed in a strong response | Accepted alternatives |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Comparative transfer across assay conditions | The repeated direction, changing magnitude, and distinction between the location and height of a rate maximum. | Different concise summaries; exact fold calculations are optional. No unsupported rate ranking at an untested condition. |
| 2 | Interpret disagreement between purified activity and cellular staining | Use substrate differences, separate localization and cytosolic comparisons, and active-enzyme availability to assess the proposed calibration. | Multiple compatible causal accounts; no unique explanation of the heme response is required. |
| 3 | Choose provisionally for a measurement objective | Weigh temporal control against recovery; identify project-specific background and yield as missing evidence. | Either initial candidate with explicit assumptions; a different objective can justify a different preference. |
| 4 | Foundational cofactor/family inference | A combination of actual sequence features, comparison with a plausible alternative, and distinction between an assignment and measured cofactor occupancy. | A justified alternative or tentative assignment that accounts for the same sequence context. |
| 5 | Foundational recognition of repeated binding loops | Both supplied loop patterns and relevant positional features, rather than acidic composition alone. | A cautious or alternative assignment that addresses both loops; measured affinity or occupancy is not required. |
| 6 | Foundational separation of anchoring and topology | The near-terminal hydrophobic region and following tail; distinguish anchor evidence from orientation or destination. | Withholding orientation, or another defensible interpretation that accounts for the sequence. |
| 7 | Foundational distinction between domains and ligand motifs | Extended similarity in two regions; distinguish a folded-domain hypothesis from a short interaction motif. | A cautious tandem-module conclusion or supported alternative family hypothesis. |
| 8 | Foundational assessment of a chromophore hypothesis | Membrane architecture, the relevant residue context, and comparison with a soluble alternative. | A cautious membrane-protein conclusion or alternative that explains why a unique assignment remains uncertain. |
| 9 | Evaluate whether a probe reveals or changes organization | Two justified comparisons, their confounding factors, and possible results that would alter the interpretation. | Different comparison priorities, including concentration dependence; no single experimental menu is mandatory. |
| 10 | Prioritize after a relative-enrichment filter | Distinguish the measured exclusion from compatible biological explanations, and localization evidence from functional evidence. | Follow-up or deferral with assumptions, competing priorities, and evidence that could reverse the decision. |

## Operational checks

Before accepting an item or response, check that:

1. Every required fact is in the student input or is identified as relevant background knowledge; hidden source passages are not prerequisites.
2. Each major sequence, number, or observation contributes to the task. No invented measurement or hypothetical outcome is presented as published evidence.
3. A response connects evidence to its conclusion. Naming a domain, proposing a comparison, or repeating a limitation without justification is insufficient.
4. Plausible alternatives receive credit when they address the evidence. The rubric does not silently turn an open question back into a single-answer test.
5. Uncertainty is specific and decision-relevant. A provisional choice can be strong; blanket refusal to conclude is not automatically strong.
6. Interpretation does not become a protocol or a new engineering claim. Proposed comparisons remain at the level requested by the question.

These are assistant source-aware revisions and assessment criteria. Difficulty, expert agreement, and learning gains have not been measured. Source notes preserve the distinction between published observations and hypothetical teaching scenarios.
