# Preparation criteria for the ten examples

The collection retains its original five selection criteria: a supported answer, fair input, a reusable biological lesson, clear reasoning, and variety. The current format is ten multiple-choice questions with A–D options and complete worked answers. These criteria guide preparation and review; they are not a student-facing grading rubric. Earlier open-response instructions are superseded.

## Original criteria and feedback

- **Supported answer:** Distinguish supplied observations, established background knowledge, and inference. A source-backed observation does not establish every proposed mechanism. The selected option must be best supported for the stated task; its explanation must preserve compatible mechanisms when the evidence does not identify a unique cause.
- **Fair input:** Supply the evidence needed for the stated task. Remove unused sequences and incidental numbers. Preserve A–D options. Make the decision objective explicit and use plausible alternatives whose differences matter scientifically. Cautious wording alone should not identify the key. If two options are equally defensible under the stated objective, revise the question rather than silently excluding one.
- **Reusable biological lesson:** Require a consequential distinction: transferring evidence across conditions, interpreting a measurement, weighing objectives, or separating a sequence hypothesis from demonstrated function. A numerical read-off alone is insufficient for the main lesson.
- **Clear reasoning:** Explain the decisive evidence, its implication, and why the other choices do not answer the stated question as well. Keep complete worked answers, while avoiding repetition and unrequested reasoning. Explanation length, generic caveats, and exhaustive lists do not establish reasoning quality.
- **Variety:** Preserve both foundational sequence interpretation and research judgment. Questions 4–8 are foundational exercises; they are not presented as advanced reasoning. Question 1 assesses comparative transfer rather than merely arithmetic.

## Operational checks

Before accepting an item, check that:

1. Every required fact is in the student input or is identified as relevant background knowledge; hidden source passages are not prerequisites.
2. Each major sequence, number, or observation contributes to the task. No invented measurement or hypothetical outcome is presented as published evidence.
3. The worked answer connects evidence to its conclusion. Naming a domain, proposing a comparison, or repeating a limitation without justification is insufficient.
4. Exactly one option is best supported under the stated task. Alternative explanations remain possible where the evidence permits them. Do not turn a context-specific decision into a universal biological or experimental rule.
5. Uncertainty is specific and decision-relevant. A provisional choice can be strong; blanket refusal to conclude is not automatically strong.
6. Interpretation does not become a protocol or a new engineering claim. Proposed comparisons remain at the level requested by the question.
7. Each question has four A–D choices and a matching answer letter. Reader pages and exported training messages agree. Reader pages contain no grading rubrics.
8. Review explicitly looks for answer-selection shortcuts, unnecessary reasoning and mismatches between the task and its background. A successful export check is not evidence that these teaching concerns are resolved.

These are assistant source-aware revisions and preparation criteria. Difficulty, expert agreement, and learning gains have not been measured. Source notes preserve the distinction between published observations and hypothetical teaching scenarios.
